The problem is not a lack of data

It is that the market has thousands of small companies and almost no attention to spare. A business can drift far out of view long before anything about it changes.

It brings names back into view

CrashDash watches UK-listed smaller companies and highlights those whose share price has fallen a long way from its high. These are the names that screens tend to miss and research rarely reaches.

It puts the context beside it

A falling price on its own tells you nothing. Alongside each name you get the company's identity, its own announcements, and how investors are talking about it — the evidence you need to form a view.

It stays out of the decision

CrashDash raises a name for research. It does not tell you to buy or sell anything, it does not predict a price, and it does not keep score. The judgement stays with you.

The idea

Most of the time, a share price that has collapsed is right. Something broke, the market noticed, and the price is doing its job. That is the ordinary case, and CrashDash assumes it.

Occasionally it is not. A company with no analyst coverage, no news flow and no liquidity can fall simply because everyone who was going to sell already has. At that point the price stops being a judgement about the business and starts being a record of who left.

Those two situations look almost identical on a chart. Telling them apart is research work — reading the announcements, understanding what the company actually does, noticing whether anyone is still paying attention.

CrashDash does not claim to resolve that ambiguity. It exists to make sure the question gets asked: to put the most extreme, least-watched names in front of you, with their evidence attached, so that a human being can decide whether any of them deserve a proper look.

The tool's job is to surface the question. The analyst's job is to answer it.

Who it's for

People who already do their own research, and who would rather be shown a candidate list than handed a conclusion.

  • Self-directed investors who follow UK smaller companies and want a systematic way to notice what has fallen out of the conversation.
  • Analysts and researchers covering small- and micro-cap markets, who need candidates surfaced rather than screened away.
  • Anyone who has ever missed a recovery because the company had been out of the news for two years before it moved.

CrashDash is built for reading, not for trading. If you are looking for signals to act on automatically, it is the wrong tool.

What CrashDash is not

Stated plainly, because it matters more than the marketing.

  • Not financial advice, and not a recommendation
  • No buy, sell or hold calls
  • No price targets or forecasts
  • No profit, loss or performance tracking
  • No execution, order routing or brokerage connection
  • No portfolio management
  • Not a substitute for your own due diligence
  • Not a prediction of what happens next

CrashDash is a research and education tool. You are responsible for any decision you make. If a company's situation is unclear, the honest answer is that it is unclear — the tool is designed to help you reach that conclusion faster, not to talk you out of it.

Questions

Does CrashDash tell me what to buy?

No. It surfaces companies that may warrant a closer look and gives you the evidence around them. Every decision, and every conclusion, is yours.

Is this financial advice?

No. CrashDash is a research and education tool. It is not regulated advice and it is not a recommendation.

Which markets does it cover?

UK-listed smaller companies. Coverage is deliberately narrow, and the beta is focused on getting the quality right before the breadth.

Will it work on my phone?

The site you are reading is fully responsive. The research product itself is designed for desktop reading, which is where this kind of work happens.

What happens to my details if I request access?

Requests are handled manually by the team. This website collects nothing itself — it holds no accounts, no analytics and no cookies.

Where does the data come from?

We do not discuss the internals of how the product is built. What matters publicly is the standard it is held to: every claim should be traceable to an observable source.
